A helpful distinction is:

- **Processes:** operations the mind performs—such as attending, remembering, comparing.
- **Representations:** forms in which information is organized—such as concepts, schemas, mental models.
- **Control mechanisms:** ways the mind directs and monitors those operations.

## 1. Working memory: keeping information available for thinking

**Working memory lets you maintain a limited amount of information while using it.**

For example, when reading:

> “Although Maria disagreed with Daniel, she accepted his proposal.”

You need to keep the people and their relationship available while interpreting what “she” and “his” refer to.

Working memory supports:
- Following sentences and arguments.
- Mental arithmetic.
- Comparing alternatives.
- Keeping a goal in mind while acting.
 

It is more than short-term storage: **holding information and manipulating it are related but distinguishable functions**.

Baddeley’s model, mentioned in your text, is influential, but not the only model. It includes the phonological loop, visuospatial sketchpad, central executive, and an **episodic buffer**, which helps integrate information across sources and long-term memory.

**Important limitation:** its capacity is constrained, but there is no single fixed number that describes every task. Prior knowledge, attention, and how information is grouped all matter.

## 2. Schemas: organized expectations based on experience

A **schema is an organized pattern of knowledge about a familiar kind of thing or situation**.

Your restaurant schema might include:
- Someone seats you.
- You choose food.
- Food arrives.
- You pay.

That knowledge helps you understand a story without needing every detail explicitly stated.

Schemas support:
- Rapid interpretation.
- Predictions.
- Filling gaps.
- Organizing new information.
- Remembering meaningful patterns.

But schemas can also mislead: you may assume something happened because it normally happens, even when it was never stated.

**Schemas are not all of long-term memory.** Long-term memory also includes personal experiences, facts, skills, and other forms of knowledge.

A related idea is a **script**: a schema organized around a typical sequence of events, such as ordering food.

## 3. Mental models: representing how a particular situation works

Schemas and mental models overlap, but a useful distinction is:

- **Schema:** general knowledge about a type of situation.
- **Mental model:** your current representation of a particular situation or system.


For example:
- Schema: how meetings generally work.
- Mental model: who disagrees with whom in *this* meeting, what each person wants, and what might happen next.

Mental models let you **simulate possibilities**:

> “If I change this part, what happens to the rest?”

During reading, a **situation model** represents the people, events, locations, goals, and causal relationships described—not merely the exact words.

## 4. Attention: selecting what gets processed

Attention helps prioritize some information over competing information.

It includes:
- **Selective attention:** focusing on something relevant.
- **Sustained attention:** maintaining engagement.
- **Shifting attention:** moving between relevant targets.

Attention and working memory cooperate, but they are not identical. You can attend to something without retaining it, and retain something that is no longer in front of you.

Also, relevance is not determined only by conscious choice: novelty, emotion, and learned significance can capture attention.

## 5. Executive control: directing thought toward a goal

Executive functions help coordinate thinking and behavior, especially when automatic responses are insufficient.

Commonly studied functions include:
- **Inhibition:** resisting an irrelevant response or distraction.
- **Updating:** replacing information that is no longer relevant.
- **Cognitive flexibility:** changing rules, perspectives, or strategies.

Planning and goal management depend on these functions, along with memory and other processes.

There is no little “manager” inside the brain. “Executive control” names a collection of interacting mechanisms.

## 6. Concepts, categories, and chunking: making complexity manageable

**Concepts** organize knowledge: “tree,” “justice,” “variable,” “cause.”

**Categorization** lets you treat different things as instances of a shared kind, rather than learning everything from scratch.

**Chunking** groups separate elements into a meaningful unit.

For an experienced reader, a familiar word functions as a unit rather than a collection of unrelated letters. A chess expert recognizes meaningful board configurations.

**Chunking does not simply enlarge working memory. It lets existing knowledge make the information more manageable.**

## 7. Reasoning: operating on representations

Some particularly useful mental operations are:

| Operation | What it enables |
|---|---|
| **Inference** | Going beyond explicitly given information |
| **Comparison** | Finding similarities and differences |
| **Abstraction** | Extracting a general pattern from examples |
| **Analogy** | Using one situation to understand another |
| **Causal reasoning** | Thinking about what produces what |
| **Counterfactual thinking** | Considering “what if things were different?” |

These are closer to “instruments” in the everyday sense: operations you can deliberately apply.

However, reasoning is not automatically accurate. It depends on your knowledge, assumptions, and the evidence available.

## 8. Metacognition: monitoring and regulating your thinking

Metacognition includes:
- **Knowledge about your cognition:** “I struggle to remember unfamiliar names.”
- **Monitoring:** “I’m not understanding this argument.”
- **Control:** “I’ll slow down and reconstruct it.”

One crucial distinction: **feeling that you understand is not the same as understanding**. Familiar wording and easy reading can produce misleading confidence.

Trying to explain an idea from memory often exposes gaps that rereading does not.

## How they work together

Suppose you read:

> “Leo put the ice cream in the car. An hour later, the seats were sticky.”

Your mind may:
1. Use **attention** to select the sentence.
2. Access **concepts** such as ice cream and cars.
3. Use **working memory** to connect the two statements.
4. Activate **schemas** about melting.
5. Make an **inference** that the ice cream melted and spilled.
6. Build a **mental model** of what happened.
7. Use **metacognition** to notice that this explanation is plausible, but not explicitly confirmed.

These processes overlap rather than forming a rigid assembly line.

## One correction to the text you received

Its overall picture is useful, but **skilled reading is not mainly guessing words from context**. Accurate, increasingly automatic word recognition is fundamental; context is especially important for interpreting meaning and resolving ambiguity.

Also, “pre-reading, during reading, post-reading” describes a useful instructional routine, not mandatory neurological stages.

**The most useful starting trio is working memory, schemas, and mental models:** what you keep available now, what organized knowledge you bring, and what representation you construct from the two. Attention and executive control guide that construction; metacognition checks how well it is working.
