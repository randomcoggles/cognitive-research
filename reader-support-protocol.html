Design the conditions for understanding

The reader-support protocol

A practical framework for content that supports attention, working memory, understanding, and motivation. Start with the reader. Make the thinking worthwhile. Remove difficulty that serves no learning purpose.

Support, not control.

This protocol is a design framework, not a guaranteed sequence of brain activity. Its matrix expresses intended support, not measured effects, neural activation, or a method for manipulating readers.

01 / Name the different kinds of things

What are we designing for?

Cognition is not a collection of interchangeable tools. The framework distinguishes systems and representations, operations on them, and reasons for engaging.

Systems & representations

Working memory keeps information available for active use. Concepts and schemas organize knowledge. A situation model represents a particular event; a conceptual model can represent how a system works.

Processes & regulation

Attention, retrieval, integration, inference, updating, executive control, and metacognitive monitoring help select, connect, use, and evaluate information. They overlap rather than form a strict assembly line.

Engagement factors

Motivation, interest, and perceived relevance influence whether continuing is worthwhile. Clear content can support engagement, but it cannot guarantee interest or persistence.

03 / A reusable section scaffold

Make one meaningful advance

Use this as a flexible checklist, not a mandatory sequence. A paragraph does not need every move; a short explanation may combine several.

1

Question & value

[What question does this section answer? Why does it matter to this reader?]

2

Starting point

[What familiar idea or prerequisite helps? Define unfamiliar terms before relying on them.]

3

Core explanation

[State one central claim. Make the relevant relationships explicit.]

4

Concrete example

[Show the claim in action. Explain which part of the example illustrates which part of the idea.]

5

Connection & boundary

[Connect to the larger picture. State what does not follow, or where the analogy stops.]

6

Optional check

[Ask for an explanation, distinction, or new application. Offer useful feedback rather than a trick question.]

7

Takeaway & next question

[Recap the advance. Resolve the current question before inviting the reader to the next one.]

Complete the brief first

  • Reader: Who is this for, and in what reading context?
  • Starting knowledge: What can I reasonably assume?
  • Purpose: What question or need brings the reader here?
  • Outcome: What should they understand or do afterward?
  • Likely difficulty: What term, relation, or assumption may cause confusion?

Do not optimize for frictionless reading alone

Some effort is valuable: comparing cases, explaining a mechanism, or revising an assumption. Remove accidental difficulty, not all intellectual challenge.

04 / Watch the protocol change the writing

Two annotated revisions

These are illustrative author decisions, not proof of a cognitive effect. A clearer passage still needs testing with its intended readers.

Example A · Explain a relationship

Before
It supplies the needed information and helps construct it. This is what improves comprehension.
After
A schema is organized prior knowledge. When you read about mixing milk, flour, and an egg, recipe knowledge can help you infer that the mixture is batter. Working memory keeps useful details active as a model of this particular event develops. The passage does not establish that the batter was cooked.

OrientDefine “schema” before using it as an explanatory concept.

ClarifyReplace ambiguous “it” with the relevant system or representation.

ShowMap a concrete recipe example to the abstract relationship.

BoundarySeparate a plausible inference from an event the text never stated.

Check: Ask the reader which detail is inferred and whether cooking was established.

Example B · Keep the goal visible

Before
Before we explain how to choose a backup interval, here are twelve storage terms. The most important secret comes at the end.
After
How much work could you afford to lose? That answer helps you choose a backup interval. For example, hourly backups can leave roughly an hour of changes unprotected between successful backups. First choose an acceptable loss window; then check whether your backup system can meet it reliably. Next we will distinguish that window from the time needed to restore service.

PurposeStart with the reader's decision, not an unrelated glossary.

ExplainIntroduce the interval and its consequence together.

BoundaryThe estimate assumes successful backups; failures can increase loss.

ContinueProvide the useful answer before introducing the next question.

Check: Ask the reader to choose an interval for a different acceptable loss window and explain the assumptions.

05 / Verify understanding, not just engagement

Close the loop with real readers

Use a representative reader, a realistic task, and a specific intended outcome. Avoid interpreting every error as a memory or attention problem.

1. Explain

Ask the reader to explain the main idea without copying the passage. Look for the essential relationships, not matching wording.

2. Transfer

Offer a new case and ask for an application. Check whether the reader can reason beyond the original example.

3. Locate confusion

Ask what remains unclear and where understanding broke down. Use errors and feedback to revise the passage, then check again.

Clicks, scroll depth, time on page, and satisfaction can inform engagement or usability. None alone establishes comprehension. Fast reading may mean fluency, skimming, or disengagement; longer reading may mean interest or confusion.

Scope: This is a practical synthesis and editable design hypothesis, not a validated psychological assessment or scored cognitive model.
